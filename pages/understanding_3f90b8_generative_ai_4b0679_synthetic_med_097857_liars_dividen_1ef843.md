## Introduction

Artificial intelligence does not only create convincing fake images and videos. It also changes how people judge authentic ones. This secondary effect is often called the **liar’s dividend**: once the public knows that realistic [synthetic media]({{ 'synthetic-media/' | relative_url }}) exists, a person confronted with genuine visual evidence can claim that it was generated or altered by AI. The result is a paradox. Improvements in fake media can weaken trust in real media. Researchers Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron, who popularised the term, argued that deepfakes may help people evade accountability for things that are actually true.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.californialawreview.org/print/deep-fakes-a-looming-challenge-for-privacy-democracy-and-national-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: californialawreview.org">[California Law Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">californialawreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">California Law ReviewDeep Fakes: A Looming Challenge for Privacy, Democracy...And, in what we call the “liar&#x27;s dividend,” deep fakes mak...</span></span></span>

Within the broader information-integrity challenge posed by synthetic images and videos, the liar’s dividend is important because it shifts [attention]({{ 'attention/' | relative_url }}) away from deception alone. The problem is not merely that fake photographs can be believed. It is also that genuine photographs can be dismissed.

## What the liar's dividend means

The liar’s dividend describes the advantage gained by someone who falsely claims that authentic evidence is fabricated. The concept emerged from concerns that increasingly realistic AI-generated media would create uncertainty about what can be trusted online.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.californialawreview.org/print/deep-fakes-a-looming-challenge-for-privacy-democracy-and-national-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: californialawreview.org">[California Law Review]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">californialawreview.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">California Law ReviewDeep Fakes: A Looming Challenge for Privacy, Democracy...And, in what we call the “liar&#x27;s dividend,” deep fakes mak...</span></span></span>

The mechanism is straightforward:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. People become aware that convincing AI-generated images and videos exist.
2. Trust in visual evidence declines.
3. When damaging evidence appears, the subject can claim it is fake.
4. Some observers become uncertain, even if the evidence is genuine.

</div>

The dividend is not the fake itself. The dividend is the benefit obtained from the possibility that a fake could exist. As awareness of deepfakes grows, the denial becomes more plausible to some audiences. Chesney and Citron noted that the phenomenon can grow alongside public education about deepfakes because greater awareness of manipulation also increases opportunities for strategic doubt.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.jstor.org/stable/26891938" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: jstor.org">[JSTOR]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">jstor.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deep Fakes: A Looming Challenge for Privacy, Democracy...by B Chesney · 2019 · Cited by 2856 — Hence what we call the liar&#x27;s divide...</span></span></span>

This dynamic is closely connected to broader trends in declining trust, [political]({{ 'political-video/' | relative_url }}) polarisation, and accusations of “fake news”. AI-generated media adds another layer by providing a technically credible explanation for dismissing inconvenient evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/liars-dividend-can-politicians-claim-misinformation-to-evade-accountability/687FEE54DBD7ED0C96D72B26606AA073" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">deepfakes, leading to what Chesney and Citron (Reference Chesney and Citron2019) term the “liar&#x27;s dividend.” While this tactic of denial...</span></span></span>

## How synthetic media weakens genuine evidence

The liar’s dividend does not require viewers to believe a specific alternative story. Often it is enough to create uncertainty.

### Doubt can be more powerful than persuasion

Traditional misinformation usually tries to convince people that a false claim is true. The liar’s dividend works differently. A person may not need others to accept a replacement explanation. They only need enough people to think, “Maybe the image was AI-generated.”

That uncertainty can delay accountability, complicate investigations, and fragment public [understanding]({{ 'understanding/' | relative_url }}) of events. UNESCO has described this broader effect as part of a “crisis of knowing”, in which authentic recordings can be dismissed simply because synthetic alternatives are known to exist.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/deepfakes-and-crisis-knowing" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unesco.org">[UNESCO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unesco.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">deepfakes and crisis knowing</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Deepfakes and the crisis of knowing27 Oct 2025 — Deepfakes and the crisis of knowing. ### Audiences become more sceptical of authentic media

Recent research suggests that the growth of synthetic media may produce a general scepticism shift. In a large-scale 2026 study on audio deepfakes, researchers found that people were not dramatically worse at identifying fake audio than before, but they had become significantly less likely to trust genuine recordings. The central problem was increasing distrust of authentic content rather than improved deception alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26136" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Eroding Trust in Real Speech: A Large-Scale Study of Human Audio Deepfake PerceptionMay 21, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 21, 2026</span></span></span>

Although the study focused on audio, the underlying concern applies to visual evidence as well. If people assume that convincing fabrication is easy, they may begin treating authentic photographs and videos with greater suspicion.

### Real images can be misidentified as AI-generated

The information environment becomes even more confusing when genuine images are incorrectly labelled as AI creations. ## Why some denials succeed

Not every claim of “that image is AI-generated” is persuasive. Several factors influence whether a denial gains traction.

### Existing distrust

Denials are more likely to work when audiences already distrust institutions, journalists, political opponents, or other sources of information. In these environments, claims that evidence was fabricated may fit pre-existing beliefs. Research on the liar’s dividend suggests that strategic accusations of fakery can help public figures maintain support among sympathetic audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ideas.repec.org/p/osf/socarx/x43ph.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ideas.repec.org">[IDEAS/RePEc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ideas.repec.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IDEAS/RePEcThe Liar&#x27;s Dividend: The Impact of Deepfakes and Fake...by KJ Schiff · 2023 · Cited by 13 — This study examines the phenomeno...</span></span></span>

### Technical complexity

Most people cannot independently verify whether a photograph or video is authentic. Digital forensics, metadata analysis, provenance systems, and source [verification]({{ 'verification-cost/' | relative_url }}) often require specialist expertise. This knowledge gap creates space for competing claims about authenticity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en-us/posts/ai-in-courts/deepfakes-evidence-authentication/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: thomsonreuters.com">[Thomson Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">thomsonreuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Thomson ReutersDeepfakes on trial: How judges are navigating AI evidence...May 8, 2025 — “I think the courts will see [the liar&#x27;s divide...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2025</span></span></span>

### Speed of information sharing

Images and videos often spread much faster than verification efforts. A denial can circulate immediately, while authentication may take days or weeks. During that period, uncertainty itself can become influential.

## What makes a denial credible or weak

Because AI-generated media is real and increasingly capable, dismissing concerns outright is not reasonable. The key question is whether a denial is supported by evidence.

A denial becomes more credible when it is accompanied by:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Independent forensic analysis.
* Evidence of manipulation or editing.
* Missing or inconsistent provenance records.
* Corroborating technical findings from multiple investigators.

## The challenge for information integrity

The liar’s dividend highlights a crucial point about artificial intelligence and information integrity: the greatest risk is not always that people believe false images. Sometimes the greater risk is that people stop believing true ones.

In this sense, synthetic media changes the role of visual evidence itself. Photographs and videos no longer function as automatic proof in the way many people once assumed. Authenticity increasingly depends on context, provenance, corroboration, and verification rather than appearance alone.
