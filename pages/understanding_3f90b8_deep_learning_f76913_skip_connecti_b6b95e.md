## Introduction

As neural networks became deeper, researchers expected performance to keep improving. Instead, they discovered a surprising problem: simply adding more layers could make a network harder to train and sometimes even less accurate. Skip connections, popularised by the 2015 ResNet (Residual Network) architecture, solved much of this optimisation difficulty by giving information and gradients a direct path through the network. Rather than forcing every layer to completely transform its input, skip connections allowed layers to focus on learning only the useful changes. This seemingly simple architectural adjustment made it practical to train networks with dozens, hundreds, and eventually even more than a thousand layers, helping unlock many later advances in artificial intelligence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv[1512.03385] Deep Residual Learning for Image RecognitionDecember 10, 2015 — by K He · 2015 · Cited by 315493 — We present a residua...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2015</span></span></span>

## Why simply stacking layers can fail

The intuitive idea behind [deep learning]({{ 'deep-learning/' | relative_url }}) is that more layers should allow a network to learn more complex representations. However, early experiments revealed a problem known as the degradation problem. Researchers found that beyond a certain depth, adding layers could increase training error rather than reduce it. Importantly, this was not merely a matter of [overfitting]({{ 'overfitting/' | relative_url }}); even on the training data itself, deeper networks could become harder to optimise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2cv-foundation.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv:1512.03385v1 [cs.CV] 10 Dec 2015December 10, 2015 — by K He · 2015 · Cited by 320310 — In this paper, we address the degradati...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2015</span></span></span>

One reason is that information and error signals must travel through many transformations during training. As gradients are propagated backwards, they can become weak or unstable, making it difficult for earlier layers to learn effectively. Deep networks also face a practical optimisation challenge: each additional layer increases the complexity of the function that training must discover.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.24036" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2GeeksforGeeks]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Res Net: Enabling Deep Convolutional Neural Networks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResNet: Enabling Deep Convolutional Neural Networks...October 28, 2025 — by X Liu · 2025 · Cited by 4 — Surprisingly, the addition...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 28, 2025</span></span></span>

A useful thought experiment illustrates the issue. Suppose a 20-layer network already performs well. A 30-layer version should, in theory, be able to achieve at least the same result by letting the extra ten layers do nothing. Yet standard architectures often failed to find this simple solution during training. The deeper model could therefore perform worse even though it contained all the capabilities of the shallower one. This observation became one of the central motivations for residual learning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv:1512.03385v1 [cs.CV] 10 Dec 2015December 10, 2015 — by K He · 2015 · Cited by 320310 — In this paper, we address the degradati...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2015</span></span></span>

## How residual learning changes the training problem

The key insight behind ResNet was that layers do not always need to learn an entirely new representation. Often, they only need to make a small adjustment to what already exists.

Instead of asking a stack of layers to learn a complete mapping from input to output, residual learning asks them to learn the difference between the desired output and the original input. In the ResNet formulation, a shortcut path carries the input forward unchanged while the main path learns a residual function. The final output is obtained by combining the two.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv:1512.03385v1 [cs.CV] 10 Dec 2015December 10, 2015 — by K He · 2015 · Cited by 320310 — In this paper, we address the degradati...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2015</span></span></span>

A simplified view of a residual block is:

y=F(x)+xy = F(x) + xy=F(x)+x

Here, the shortcut contributes the original signal xxx, while the learned component F(x)F(x)F(x) only has to represent what should change. If the best action is effectively to leave the signal alone, the residual function can approach zero and the block behaves close to an [identity mapping]({{ 'identity-mapping/' | relative_url }}). This is far easier for optimisation algorithms to discover than forcing several layers to learn the identity transformation from scratch.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/pdf/1512.03385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2ICML]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv:1512.03385v1 [cs.CV] 10 Dec 2015December 10, 2015 — by K He · 2015 · Cited by 320310 — In this paper, we address the degradati...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2015</span></span></span>

The shortcut connection also creates a more direct route for gradients during backpropagation. Error signals can flow through the identity path without being repeatedly distorted by every weight layer, making training more stable as depth increases. Later theoretical analyses linked this behaviour to improved gradient preservation and more reliable optimisation in very deep networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.07477" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2abhik.ai]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Norm-Preservation: Why Residual Networks Can Become Extremely Deep?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Norm-Preservation: Why Residual Networks Can Become Extremely Deep?</span></span></span>

### What the shortcut is actually doing

A common misunderstanding is that skip connections allow the network to skip learning. In reality, they make learning more selective.

The residual branch can still learn complex transformations when they are useful. The shortcut simply ensures that information already present in the input is not unnecessarily destroyed or reconstructed. Layers can therefore concentrate on refining features rather than repeatedly rebuilding them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cv-foundation.org/openaccess/content_cvpr_2016/papers/He_Deep_Residual_Learning_CVPR_2016_paper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cv-foundation.org">[cv-foundation.org+2arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cv-foundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">He Deep Residual Learning CVPR 2016 paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">We explicitly reformulate...Read more...</span></span></span>

This changes the optimisation landscape. Instead of every layer carrying the burden of preserving useful information while simultaneously creating new features, the shortcut preserves the baseline signal and the learned branch focuses on improvement. The result is a network that is easier to train even as depth grows dramatically.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cv-foundation.org/openaccess/content_cvpr_2016/papers/He_Deep_Residual_Learning_CVPR_2016_paper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cv-foundation.org">[cv-foundation.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cv-foundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">He Deep Residual Learning CVPR 2016 paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">We explicitly reformulate...Read more...</span></span></span>

## Why ResNet mattered beyond one benchmark

The original ResNet work demonstrated that residual learning could successfully train networks far deeper than those that had previously been practical. The architecture achieved leading results in the ImageNet image-recognition competition and showed that depth could continue to provide benefits when paired with the right optimisation strategy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cv-foundation.org/openaccess/content_cvpr_2016/papers/He_Deep_Residual_Learning_CVPR_2016_paper.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cv-foundation.org">[cv-foundation.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cv-foundation.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">He Deep Residual Learning CVPR 2016 paper</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">We explicitly reformulate...Read more...</span></span></span>

Its influence extended far beyond image classification. Residual connections became a standard design pattern across deep learning because they addressed a general optimisation problem rather than a task-specific one. Variants of the idea appeared in later computer vision systems, [language models]({{ 'language-models/' | relative_url }}), reinforcement-learning systems, and scientific AI applications. Surveys of modern architectures consistently identify skip connections as one of the foundational innovations that enabled extremely deep neural networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/html/2405.01725v1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Development of Skip Connection in Deep Neural Networks...2 May 2024 — This survey provides a comprehensive summary and outlook on t...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2024</span></span></span>

Perhaps the most important legacy of skip connections is conceptual. They showed that improving neural networks is not only about adding more layers or more parameters. Sometimes the decisive breakthrough comes from changing the way information flows through a model. By allowing layers to learn useful corrections instead of complete reconstructions, residual learning turned depth from a liability into an advantage and made very deep networks practical for modern artificial intelligence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv+2cv-foundation.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv[1512.03385] Deep Residual Learning for Image RecognitionDecember 10, 2015 — by K He · 2015 · Cited by 315493 — We present a residua...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 10, 2015</span></span></span>
